1. Must-Know Ecological Laws

Essential ecological laws frequently tested in definitions, direct applications, and match-the-following questions.

1. Liebig’s Law of the Minimum

Justus von Liebig MCQ: Scarcest essential nutrient limits growth

Core Idea: Growth is limited by the essential resource that is in shortest supply relative to the organism's needs, rather than by the total amount of resources available.

Example: A plant has abundant water, carbon dioxide, and sunlight, but soil nitrogen is deficient. Nitrogen limits its growth regardless of how much additional water or sunlight is supplied.

Exam Tip: Look for scenarios where increasing non-limiting resources produces no additional growth until the single deficient factor is corrected.

2. Shelford’s Law of Tolerance

Victor Ernest Shelford MCQ: Minimum limit + optimum range + maximum limit

Core Idea: An organism survives, grows, and reproduces only within a specific tolerable range of an environmental gradient, bounded by distinct ecological minimum and maximum limits.

Example: A fish species may die if water temperature rises too high or drops too low, even when food and dissolved oxygen are plentiful.
Important Distinction: Outside the tolerance range (the zone of intolerance), survival or reproduction becomes impossible; the law does not imply that every population instantly drops to zero the second a condition crosses a boundary, but rather that sustained persistence is unviable.

3. Competitive Exclusion Principle (Gause’s Law)

Georgy Gause MCQ: Complete competitors cannot coexist indefinitely

Core Idea: Two species competing for the exact same limiting resource while occupying identical ecological niches cannot coexist indefinitely under constant environmental conditions. One will outcompete and exclude the other.

Example: In laboratory cultures, two protozoan species (Paramecium aurelia and Paramecium caudatum) grown together on a single limiting bacterial food source result in the competitive exclusion of P. caudatum.

Coexistence Mechanism: In nature, competing species coexist primarily by niche differentiation (partitioning resources, utilizing different microhabitats, or shifting activity times).

4. Darwin’s Theory of Natural Selection

Charles Darwin & Alfred Russel Wallace MCQ: Heritable variation + differential reproduction

Core Idea: Individuals possessing heritable traits that enhance their survival and reproductive success in a given environment tend to leave more surviving offspring. Over successive generations, those advantageous traits become more prevalent in the population.

Example: Better-camouflaged insects avoid visually hunting predators more frequently and reproduce more, causing cryptic coloration alleles to increase across generations.
Common Trap: Individual organisms do not evolve or adapt intentionally during their lifetime; evolution by natural selection is a population-level process occurring over generational time.

5. Mendel’s Law of Segregation

Gregor Mendel MCQ: Two alleles separate during gamete formation

Core Idea: Each diploid individual possesses two alleles for any particular gene. These two alleles segregate (separate) during gamete formation (meiosis), such that each gamete carries only one allele with equal probability.

Example: A heterozygous organism with genotype Aa produces 50% gametes carrying allele A and 50% carrying allele a.

6. Mendel’s Law of Independent Assortment

Gregor Mendel MCQ: Unlinked gene pairs assort independently

Core Idea: Alleles of two or more different genes assort independently of one another during gamete formation, provided the genes are located on different chromosomes or are far apart on the same chromosome.

Example: In a dihybrid cross involving unlinked loci (AaBb), four distinct gametic combinations (AB, Ab, aB, ab) are produced in equal 1:1:1:1 proportions.
Exception / Trap: Strongly linked genes located close together on the same chromosome do not assort independently unless genetic recombination (crossing over) occurs between them.

7. Hardy–Weinberg Principle

G. H. Hardy & Wilhelm Weinberg MCQ: Genetic equilibrium in absence of evolutionary forces

Core Idea: Allele and genotype frequencies in a population remain constant across generations (genetic equilibrium) in the absence of evolutionary influences.

Allele Frequencies: p + q = 1
Genotype Frequencies: p² + 2pq + q² = 1

Required Assumptions: (1) Infinitely large population size, (2) Completely random mating, (3) No mutation, (4) No gene flow (no migration), and (5) No natural selection.

Disrupting Factors: Small population size (genetic drift), non-random mating, gene flow, mutations, and natural selection violate equilibrium and drive evolutionary change.

8. Lindeman’s 10% Law

Raymond Lindeman MCQ: Energy transfer efficiency between trophic levels

Core Idea: In simplified trophic dynamics, approximately 10% of the energy assimilated at one trophic level is transferred to and stored as biomass in the next trophic level. Approximately 90% is dissipated as heat through respiration, metabolic maintenance, or unconsumed waste.

Example: In an idealized food chain, 10,000 kJ of solar energy assimilated by primary producers supports roughly 1,000 kJ at the primary consumer level and 100 kJ at the secondary consumer level.
Rule of Thumb: 10% is an empirical generalization and ecological rule of thumb, not an invariant universal constant across all ecosystems.

2. Other Ecological Laws and Named Rules

Geographical, morphological, and ecological rules describing broad evolutionary and ecological patterns.

Law / Rule Main Concept to Remember Typical MCQ Clue
Bergmann’s Rule Within broadly distributed taxonomic clades of endothermic animals, populations and species of larger body size are found in colder climates, whereas smaller-bodied species inhabit warmer regions (lower surface-area-to-volume ratio minimizes heat dissipation). Body size and climate / latitude
Allen’s Rule Endothermic animals living in cold climates tend to have shorter limbs, ears, tails, and external appendages than closely related forms from warmer climates to minimize convective heat loss. Ears, limbs, and appendage length
Gloger’s Rule Within related groups of warm-blooded animals, more heavily pigmented (darker) individuals tend to be found in warm, humid environments, whereas paler forms occur in arid or cold environments. Color pigmentation and humidity
Jordan’s Rule In many teleost fish taxa, species or populations inhabiting colder, higher-latitude waters tend to have higher vertebral counts than related forms living in warm waters. Fish vertebrae number and water temperature
Rapoport’s Rule Species’ geographical range sizes tend to increase at higher latitudes, meaning tropical species generally possess narrower latitudinal ranges than temperate and polar species. Latitude and geographical range size
Species–Area Relationship The number of species found in a habitat increases as a power function of the area sampled (S = cA^z). Larger continuous areas support larger populations and more microhabitats. Habitat area and species richness
Island Biogeography Theory Formulated by MacArthur and Wilson. Species richness on an island represents a dynamic equilibrium between immigration rate (governed primarily by distance from mainland) and extinction rate (governed primarily by island area). Island size, isolation, immigration, and extinction
Ecological Release A species expands its realized niche, habitat breadth, or resource utilization when released from interspecific competition or predation pressure (often observed in island colonizers). Niche expansion under reduced competition
Allee Effect A phenomenon where per-capita population growth rate, individual fitness, or reproductive success declines at critically low population sizes or densities (e.g. difficulty locating mates, breakdown of social defenses). Low density leading to reproductive decline
Founder Effect The loss of genetic variation that occurs when a new colony is established by a very small number of individuals originating from a larger ancestral population. Small colonizing group with non-representative gene pool
Bottleneck Effect A sharp, sudden reduction in population size caused by an environmental disaster, disease outbreak, or human persecution, resulting in severe loss of genetic diversity via random genetic drift. Sudden population crash causing genetic loss
Mass–Energy Conservation Fundamental physical law in ecology: chemical elements and matter cycle indefinitely through biogeochemical pools, whereas energy flows unidirectionally through food webs and dissipates as metabolic heat. Nutrient cycling vs unidirectional energy flow

Note: Bergmann’s, Allen’s, Gloger’s, and Rapoport’s rules represent broad biogeographical generalizations with known taxonomic exceptions, rather than strict universal laws.

3. Population and Community Ecology Principles

Core mathematical models, population growth curves, and community dynamics to memorize.

Principle / Concept Core Meaning & Exam Application
Malthusian Principle Populations possess the intrinsic capacity to expand exponentially when resources are unlimited, while food and resource production generally increases more slowly, inevitably creating resource pressure.
Exponential Growth Idealized continuous population increase in an unlimited environment: dN/dt = rN. Produces a classic J-shaped curve where growth rate accelerates as population size N increases.
Logistic Growth Density-dependent growth that incorporates environmental resistance: dN/dt = rN(1 - N/K). Produces a classic S-shaped (sigmoid) curve where growth rate slows as population approaches carrying capacity K.
Carrying Capacity (K) The maximum sustainable population size of a species that a given environment can support over time without degrading the resource base.
Lotka–Volterra Model A system of coupled differential equations modeling the reciprocal, coupled cyclical population oscillations of interacting predator and prey populations.
r/K Selection Theory Life-history framework: r-selected species emphasize high reproductive rates, small body size, early maturity, and many offspring in fluctuating environments; K-selected species emphasize competitive ability, large body size, high parental investment, and few offspring near carrying capacity.
Metapopulation Concept A regional network of spatially separated local populations of the same species connected by occasional dispersal, maintained by a balance of local patch extinctions and recolonizations.
Source–Sink Dynamics High-quality source patches produce a net demographic surplus of individuals that disperse into lower-quality sink patches, where local mortality exceeds reproduction and the population persists only through immigration.
Ecological Succession The progressive, predictable change in species composition, community structure, and ecosystem properties over time following the exposure of new ground (primary) or disturbance (secondary).
Keystone Species Concept A species that exerts a disproportionately strong structuring effect on community composition, diversity, and physical architecture relative to its numerical abundance or biomass.
Trophic Cascade Indirect ecological effects triggered by the addition or removal of top apex predators that propagate downward through intermediate trophic levels to alter plant abundance or ecosystem properties.
Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis Hypothesizes that local species richness peaks at intermediate frequencies or intensities of disturbance, where neither competitive exclusion nor severe disturbance mortality dominates.
Density-Dependent Regulation Factors whose proportional impact on population vital rates changes with population density (e.g. intra-specific competition, contagious disease transmission, territorial crowding).
Density-Independent Effects Environmental shocks whose per-capita mortality rate is independent of population density (e.g. catastrophic flash floods, severe hard freezes, wildfires).

4. Biodiversity, Evolution and Conservation Principles

Crucial concepts regarding speciation, population genetics, and conservation intervention standards.

Principle / Concept Main Concept How It May Be Asked
Biological Species Concept Defines species as groups of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups. Interbreeding and reproductive isolation
Ecological Species Concept Delineates species by their ecological niche, environmental adaptations, and the specific resource spectrum they exploit. Niche-based species definition
Morphological Species Concept Distinguishes species based on shared, measurable structural and phenotypic anatomical characteristics. Visible morphological similarity
Genetic Drift Stochastic, random fluctuations in allele frequencies across generations due to sampling error, exerting dominant effects in small populations. Random allele frequency fluctuations
Gene Flow The transfer of genetic variation between populations through the immigration and successful reproduction of individuals. Immigration and inter-population exchange
Inbreeding Depression Decreased biological fitness, survival, and reproductive output resulting from matings between close genetic relatives, exposing deleterious recessive alleles. Mating between relatives reducing fitness
Genetic Rescue The mitigation of inbreeding depression and restoration of fitness achieved by translocating individuals from a distinct outbred population into an inbred population. Introducing new alleles to reverse inbreeding
Biodiversity Hotspot Criteria Formal scientific criteria (Myers et al.) requiring: (1) at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species (>0.5% of world total) and (2) at least 70% loss of primary natural vegetation. High plant endemism + severe habitat loss
Minimum Viable Population The smallest isolated population size estimated to have a specified probability of persistence (e.g. 99%) over a given timeframe (e.g. 1,000 years). Quantitative threshold for extinction risk
Extinction Vortex A compounding, self-reinforcing downward spiral where small population size causes inbreeding and drift, reducing fitness and demographic rates, which shrinks population size further. Self-reinforcing feedback loop of decline
Habitat Fragmentation The process whereby a continuous expanse of natural habitat is transformed into multiple smaller, isolated habitat fragments divided by an altered human matrix. Subdivision into smaller, isolated patches
Edge Effect Distinct microclimatic changes (increased wind, altered solar radiation, moisture loss) and biological shifts occurring at the interface between fragmented habitat and surrounding matrix. Boundary vs interior environmental differences
Mitigation Hierarchy The mandatory sequential prioritization framework in environmental planning: (1) Avoid impacts first, (2) Minimise unavoidable impacts, (3) Restore degraded areas, and (4) Offset residual significant impacts as a last resort. Correct sequence: Avoid -> Minimise -> Restore -> Offset

5. Sustainability and Environmental Principles

Governance, environmental economics, and sustainability frameworks tested in exams.

Principle / Concept Explanation & Significance MCQ Clue
Precautionary Principle Where there are credible threats of serious or irreversible environmental harm, the lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason to postpone proportionate preventive action. Scientific uncertainty is no excuse to delay prevention
Polluter Pays Principle The party responsible for generating environmental pollution must internalize the full social and economic costs of pollution prevention, abatement, and ecological restoration. Internalizing costs of environmental damage
Prevention Principle Anticipating and stopping environmental damage at its source before it occurs is fundamentally preferable and more cost-effective than attempting cleanup or remediation afterward. Prevent harm rather than remediate
Intergenerational Equity The ethical mandate that the present human generation holds the Earth in common trust and must manage resources so that future generations inherit an intact, viable planet. Fairness across generations
Intragenerational Equity Justice, fairness, and non-discrimination in the allocation of environmental benefits, access to natural resources, and exposure to environmental pollution burdens among people living today. Fairness among people living today
CBDR Principle Common but Differentiated Responsibilities: all nations share responsibility for protecting the global environment, but historical contributions and socioeconomic capacities differ, dictating differentiated commitments. Shared responsibility, different capacities
Tragedy of the Commons Economic dilemma where individual users acting independently in their self-interest deplete or degrade a shared, open-access common-pool resource, undermining the collective welfare. Overexploitation of shared open-access resources
IPAT Equation A master conceptual framework describing environmental impact: I = P × A × T (Impact = Population × Affluence / per capita consumption × Technology intensity). Formula linking population, affluence, and technology
Precaution vs Prevention Precaution applies when a credible risk exists but scientific knowledge is incomplete or uncertain; Prevention applies to known, scientifically established, or quantifiable environmental hazards. Uncertain risk vs known, established risk
Natural Capital The world's stock of natural assets (forests, water, living biodiversity, atmosphere, and soils) that produce a continuous flow of valuable ecosystem goods and services. Ecosystems and biodiversity viewed as capital assets
Strong Sustainability Maintains that key natural capital assets provide critical, life-supporting ecological functions that cannot be substituted by human-made, industrial, or financial capital. Natural capital is non-substitutable
Weak Sustainability Assumes that total capital stock across generations is what matters, permitting substitution between natural capital and manufactured or technological capital. Natural and manufactured capital are substitutable
Circular Economy An industrial and economic model restorative and regenerative by design, eliminating waste through continuous closed loops of reuse, remanufacture, and biological recycling. Eliminating linear take-make-waste cycles
Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) A formal legal procedure to predict, evaluate, and design mitigation measures for the biophysical and social consequences of a proposed development prior to authorization. Anticipating project impacts before approval
Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) A standardized cradle-to-grave accounting methodology evaluating all environmental burdens associated with a product, from raw material extraction through disposal. Cradle-to-grave product footprint
Additionality The requirement that the carbon sequestration or biodiversity benefit of an intervention is genuinely additional to what would have occurred under a business-as-usual baseline. Would the benefit occur anyway without intervention?
Adaptive Management A structured, iterative management cycle of "learning by doing", where management actions are treated as experiments, outcomes are rigorously monitored, and policies are adjusted based on findings. Iterative cycle of monitoring and adjusting actions
